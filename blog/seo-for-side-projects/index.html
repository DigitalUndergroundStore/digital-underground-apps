SEO for side projects: a beginner's plan you can finish in a weekend

Digital UndergroundOctober 27, 2026 · 5 min read

SEO for side projects sounds like a job for someone with a marketing budget, but most of what matters is basic, unglamorous work you can do in a weekend. Search traffic is slow to start, and that is exactly why it is worth starting early: a page you write today can still bring visitors in a year, long after a social post has stopped working. Here is a practical plan.

Why search is worth it for small projects

Social posts and launches create spikes. Search creates a slow, steady stream of people who are actively looking for a solution. For a side project, that steady stream is often more valuable than a spike, because it keeps working while you are busy with your day job. You do not need to rank for big, competitive words. You need to show up for the specific phrases your users type when they have the problem you solve.

Step 1: Fix the technical basics

Search engines need to be able to find, read and understand your pages. Check these first:

  • Each page has a unique title and meta description. The title shows up as the blue link in search results, so make it clear and specific.
  • Your important content is in the HTML, not only loaded by JavaScript after the page opens. If you use a single-page app framework, consider pre-rendering your landing page and key pages.
  • You have a sitemap (a file listing your pages) and a robots.txt file that does not block search engines by accident.
  • The site works well on phones and loads reasonably fast. Compress large images and avoid loading things you do not need.
  • Each page has one clear H1 heading that says what the page is about.

Then add your site to Google Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools. Both are free, let you submit your sitemap, and show you which searches already bring people to your site.

Step 2: Find long-tail keywords your users actually type

Long-tail keywords are longer, more specific phrases, like "offline invoice generator for freelancers" instead of "invoice app". Fewer people search them, but those who do know what they want, and there is much less competition. Ways to find them without paid tools:

  • Use search autocomplete. Start typing your problem into Google and note the suggestions. Check the "People also ask" box and the related searches at the bottom of the results.
  • Read the questions people ask in forums, Discord servers and Q&A sites in your niche. The exact wording they use is often the wording they search.
  • Look at what competitors' pages are titled. Not to copy them, but to see which problems they think people search for.
  • Check Search Console after a few weeks. It shows real queries where your pages appeared, including ones you never thought of.

Pick a short list of phrases where you can honestly offer the best answer. One clear topic per page works better than one page trying to rank for everything.

Step 3: Write a few genuinely helpful pages

You do not need a hundred blog posts. A handful of strong pages beats a pile of thin ones. Good options for side projects:

  • A focused landing page for each main use case, using the words your users search.
  • How-to guides that solve the problem fully, even for readers who never use your product. Mention your tool where it naturally fits.
  • Comparison or alternatives pages that honestly explain how your project differs from the common options.
  • Templates, checklists or free tools related to your product. These attract both visitors and links.

For each page, put the main phrase in the title, the H1, the URL and the first paragraph, then write naturally. Answer the question quickly, use clear headings, and cut filler. If a reader would leave satisfied, you are on the right track.

Step 4: Link your pages together

Internal links (links from one of your pages to another) help search engines understand which pages matter and help visitors find the next useful thing. Link from guides to the relevant product page, from the home page to your best guides, and between related articles. Use descriptive link text such as "how to price your side project" rather than "click here".

Step 5: Earn your first links

Links from other sites still act as a signal of trust. For a side project, the realistic sources are:

  • Directories and marketplaces that fit your category, where each listing is its own page.
  • Your own profiles: GitHub, your social bios, maker communities and forum signatures where allowed.
  • Helpful answers in communities, when linking is welcome and the link really answers the question.
  • Guest posts or interviews on small blogs and newsletters in your niche. They are usually happy to feature a maker with a real story.

Avoid buying links or joining link schemes. They can do more harm than good, and they are not worth the risk for a small site.

Step 6: Measure and be patient

New pages often take weeks or months to settle in search results. Check Search Console every couple of weeks rather than every day. Look for pages that get impressions but few clicks (improve the title and description), and searches you appear for but do not fully answer (expand the page or write a new one). Update your best pages when your product changes, so they stay accurate.

A weekend SEO checklist

  1. Saturday morning: fix titles, descriptions, H1s and the sitemap. Add the site to Search Console.
  2. Saturday afternoon: collect ten long-tail phrases and pick the best three.
  3. Sunday: write one helpful guide and improve your main landing page around one phrase.
  4. Sunday evening: add internal links and submit your project to two relevant directories.

Then repeat the writing step once or twice a month. Small, steady effort compounds.
